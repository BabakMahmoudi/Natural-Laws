# Accidental Regularities and the Ontology of Laws

**Source dialogue:** [Laws, Accidents, and Events](../Dialogs/Laws-Accidents-and-Events.md)

## Question

Does the difficulty of distinguishing accidental regularities from laws give us reason to posit laws as features of the world? What, if anything, does successful prediction or compression add to that case?

## Core argument

Babak’s challenge begins with a separation between events and our accounts of them. Events occur; we formulate statements, classify patterns, and use some formulations to predict. Calling one generalization a law and another an accident is initially a distinction made within that practice. Difficulty applying the distinction is an epistemic or classificatory difficulty. It does not, by itself, show that the world contains an additional property of lawhood.

The same constraint applies to scientific success. A description may capture many events compactly and support reliable predictions. This establishes something about the fit between the description and the events observed. The inference from that fit to a governing law, necessary connection, or other ontological addition requires a separate argument. Compressibility is a feature of a representation relative to a scheme of description; it cannot simply be identified with a law existing in nature.

This is a limited conclusion. It does not establish that laws, dispositions, or modal structures do not exist. Nor does it show that all classifications are arbitrary. It blocks an inference from our descriptive and predictive practices alone to an ontology of laws.

## How the dialogue developed

The initial suggestion was that the law–accident distinction must be grounded in something that makes a regularity a law. The example of silver coins in a drawer tried to explain an accident through a true general statement that gives us no reason to expect the next coin to be silver. Babak observed that “statement,” “reason,” and “expectation” belong to language and cognition. The example thus failed to isolate a mind-independent mark of accidentalness. The AI conceded the shift from ontology to epistemology.

Babak then proposed the stronger picture: “out there, there are only events”; signs, statements, prediction, and compression belong to our activity of describing them. A subsequent DeepSeek critique correctly noted that “only events” is itself a substantive ontological thesis, not a consequence of the epistemic argument. It also asked how a best-system account, dispositional realism, and the success of prediction bear on the issue. The dialogue ended with the narrower conclusion intact and these larger questions open.

## Positions and objections to examine

**Best-system Humeanism.** On the Mill–Ramsey–Lewis approach, laws belong to the best systematization of the total distribution of particular facts, commonly assessed by simplicity and strength. This offers a criterion for lawhood without a governing entity. The open issue is whether “best” is sufficiently objective: its success depends on how the world is, while simplicity may depend on language and choice of basic predicates. It is too quick to declare the entire distinction merely subjective.

**Nomic or dispositional realism.** A realist may hold that events involve capacities, dispositions, or modal relations that ground laws and distinguish them from coincidences. This is a possible independent answer to Babak’s challenge, not something refuted by pointing out that prediction is a mental activity. The realist must articulate and defend that additional structure.

**Prediction and projectibility.** Why do some compact formulations continue to predict well? Asking this is legitimate, but the question must not presuppose that their future success is guaranteed or requires a lawlike entity. A world of events might exhibit extensive patterns as a brute fact. Whether that account is satisfactory remains unsettled.

## Open problems

1. Can “only events” be defended positively, rather than used as a working proposal?
2. Are there objective patterns among events without objective lawhood? What makes a pattern objective on this account?
3. Can the best-system criterion distinguish laws from accidents without making lawhood depend on contingent choices of language?
4. What independent argument could establish dispositional or modal structure?
5. What does a nonrealist account of laws permit scientists to claim about unobserved cases and future events?

## Provisional conclusion

The accidental-regularity challenge must keep three questions separate: what happens in the world, how we describe and predict it, and whether anything beyond events grounds certain patterns. The dialogue establishes a constraint on inference, not a final ontology: neither classificatory difficulty nor predictive compression alone proves the existence of laws.
