# The World We Keep in View

*AI-generated draft (model: GPT-6.1); developed from [a dialogue](../Dialogs/2026-10-30-kant-causation-and-predictive-world.md) and [memo](../Memos/2026-10-30-causation-as-working-assumption.md) in this inquiry. Not yet reviewed or endorsed by the human contributor.*

A cup disappears behind a book. We do not ordinarily experience its disappearance as the destruction of the cup, followed by its recreation when the book moves. We expect it to remain there. We can reach behind the book, and if our fingers meet the handle, that result belongs to the same world in which we first saw the cup. What sustains this world across the interval in which sight is silent? And what, if anything, does that continuity tell us about the world's laws?

Kant's question in the Second Analogy of the *Critique of Pure Reason* concerns the difference between a succession in my perceptions and my perception of an objective succession. I may look at the roof of a house and then at its door, without thinking the roof existed before the door. When I judge that a boat moved downstream, I take its positions to form an order belonging to the boat. Kant argues that an objective alteration must be represented as occurring according to a causal rule. The rule does not have to be known in its particular physical form for this general requirement to apply. His argument concerns the conditions for experience of an objective event, rather than an observation of an invisible bond between events.

The challenge begins where a quick reading of that argument can overreach. Suppose we need causal order to maintain an intelligible world. Why should a necessity in our experience or judgment be a necessity governing a physical reality independently of us? The distinction is especially vivid in a dream. Things happen there; a person may even accept their order while dreaming. On waking, the dream's apparent world has not endured the tests to which an ordinary cup remains subject. Yet the dream prevents us from saying simply that subjective experience has no events and objective experience has them. We need to ask what makes a model of a world dependable, and what kind of warrant its dependability supplies.

Contemporary predictive-processing theories offer a striking way to sharpen that question. In influential predictive-coding models, higher levels of a neural hierarchy generate expectations about activity at lower levels. Differences between expected and incoming signals—prediction errors—help update the model. “Prediction” need not mean a conscious guess about tomorrow. An interpretation of what is present now can predict what further sensory signals should accompany it. If a cup is partly hidden, its apparent shape suggests what should appear if the book is moved. Friston's broader account treats perception as inference about conditions that could produce sensory input; Rao and Ballard offered an explicit hierarchical model of visual processing. These are scientific proposals, not established descriptions of every perceptual circuit. [Rao and Ballard 1999; Friston 2005]

The proposal developed here is that this **persistent, corrigible internal model** may provide a contemporary way to think about what Kant calls objective experience. This is an analogy and reconstruction, not an attribution to Kant of a theory of neurons. The model presents objects that persist when unseen, events that occur in an order, and relations that allow expectations about what an intervention will reveal. In the ordinary sense, we experience the cup, not a consciously inspected model of a cup. Calling the organization “internal” is a claim about a proposed mechanism of experience, not a reason to deny that perception reaches a world.

A creature relying on such a model must be able to act before it has complete evidence. It reaches behind the book, avoids the fire, and expects a released object to fall. Causal assumptions earn their place through this practical work. They allow the creature to carry information from one encounter to another and to correct a failed expectation without starting its world anew. A lawlike relationship need not mean perfect prediction: weather, other animals, and even familiar objects can surprise us. The relevant achievement is a model stable enough to guide action and flexible enough to register error.

The image of natural laws as rules governing how events evolve can itself be adopted as a useful model. It supports prediction, intervention, and inquiry. Its usefulness is the claim being made here; no inference from that success to the existence of additional governing entities is needed. One can work within this causal framework while remaining agnostic about its literal metaphysics. This practical stance has an affinity with Carnap's suggestion that the adoption of a conceptual framework can be evaluated by its fruitfulness, though it does not settle every question posed within that framework.

A different, more speculative picture asks what a law might be in the physical world if it is not an extra rule directing events. In a block-universe picture, the full histories of objects form a structured whole. Laws may describe its symmetries and regularities: a particle's constant-velocity trajectory has a shape that a concise statement captures. The copy-universe thought experiment makes the point vivid. If every event and trajectory in a copied block coincides with the original, what further law would a creator have to copy? This descriptive account is our current best guess about the ontology of laws, conditional on a block view of time; it is neither established by predictive neuroscience nor required for the practical use of causal rules. “Symmetry” may also be too narrow to cover all lawlike structure.

There is also a difficulty for the proposal itself. A model might succeed locally while badly misdescribing what produces its inputs. Different models can support the same predictions; a dream can briefly sustain an apparent order; and predictive coding, as a specific neural theory, has contested empirical reach. Nor does an account of how a brain constructs experience settle Kant's stronger question about what must be true for objective experience to be possible at all. If a creature could build an objective, corrigible world without representing every alteration under a causal rule, Kant's proposed necessity would need revision. If it could not, that still would not automatically establish a necessity in things considered apart from possible experience.

The two claims can thus stand at different levels. For inquiry and survival, treating laws as governing rules is a practical and useful assumption. As a tentative account of what laws amount to in a block universe, they describe symmetries or patterns in its complete history rather than independently directing that history. This second claim remains a best guess, dependent on how we understand time and on whether a descriptive account can explain the apparent modal force of laws. We can continue to use the governing model without settling that deeper question.
