# Copying a Universe: What Must Be Copied for a Law to Be There?

*Developed from [The Law and the Shape of a World](../Dialogs/2026-10-29-law-and-shape-of-a-world.md) and [Laws as Descriptions of a Complete History](../Memos/Laws-as-Descriptions-of-a-Complete-History.md). This essay explores a proposal; it does not present a new theory of laws.*

Suppose we commission a maker to copy a small part of the universe: a sealed region containing two particles, A and B, moving at constant velocities. We ask for more than a model that looks right in a photograph. We want the region itself, including its motions, recreated. What exactly must the maker put there?

The particles seem straightforward enough. We can ask whether they occupy the right places and have the right properties. Yet when we add, “And copy the law that keeps them moving uniformly,” our instructions become less clear. Where should the maker find this law, and what would count as installing it? One answer is to specify a rule in a simulation. But a simulation's program is a further physical object whose operations produce representations of motion. It does not follow that the copied region itself needs a comparable inscription or mechanism. The question is about what belongs in the world we are copying, rather than what a maker might use to manufacture it.

Let the particles' positions at times t₁ through tₙ form two trajectories. If the task is to copy the *whole* history, the maker must reproduce those trajectories at every time. We can then measure their geometry: equal changes of position in equal intervals, in this simplified example. A statement of constant velocity describes that arrangement economically. If we find every particle, every position, and every temporal relation faithfully reproduced, what further item remains for the maker to copy? Asking this does not establish that there is no further item. It puts the burden on an account that posits one to tell us what it is and how its presence would make a difference to the copy.

A complication appears if the maker returns at an intermediate moment tₘ and announces success. We see A at aₘ and B at bₘ. Suppose we also have perfect records of their earlier positions. Those records cannot tell us whether the later positions match. The two regions might have identical histories so far and diverge tomorrow. If our assignment includes the entire trajectory, the claim of success cannot yet be checked by inspecting only the present and past. A proposed law at tₘ might be offered as a guarantee. But that answer is precisely what our inquiry is testing: is the guarantee an additional feature of the region, or a confident statement about a future we have not observed?

Here an account of time enters the experiment. In a block picture, past and future events belong to one complete temporal structure. The maker's assignment can be understood as copying that structure, including what an inhabitant at tₘ calls the future. We at tₘ might lack access to those later events, but our ignorance would not make them absent from the copy. The law can be read as a description of the completed trajectories. This picture makes a descriptive account of laws intelligible; it does not entail it. Someone may still argue that a further feature explains why the complete trajectories have this form.

On a picture in which the future is not yet real, “copy the whole history now” is a different, perhaps incoherent, instruction. We might instead ask the maker to reproduce the present and ensure that it develops appropriately. Then the question of what secures the next positions presses harder. A governing law, a particle's intrinsic power, or some other source of continuation may seem necessary. But the appeal to an open future alone cannot decide among those answers. It changes what the copying task asks and where the explanatory gap appears. Conversely, the block picture does not banish the question of why its whole structure has just this shape.

The experiment therefore has two distinct tests. A **completeness test** asks what an exact copy of the whole world must contain. A **continuation test** asks what would make a match up to tₘ extend beyond tₘ. They should not be silently exchanged. The first may encourage us to regard laws as descriptions of a history; the second invites an account of production or dependence. Even if a proposed law answers the second, we should still ask what it adds to the first.

Think of a blind person climbing an unfamiliar staircase. From the steps already encountered, the climber learns something about the staircase's form and plans the next movement. Knowledge of the pattern matters enormously. It does not put the next step in place. Similarly, a law can organize what we have found and help us anticipate what we have not yet seen without itself pushing a particle to its next position. The analogy has a limit: the staircase already has steps beyond the climber's reach, whereas whether the future exists independently of our experience is the very point at issue. That limit makes the temporal question visible instead of settling it by metaphor.

Nor should the word *symmetry* do more work than it can bear. Uniform motion is a particularly transparent geometric pattern, but the laws used in science include statistical claims and relations that may not look like simple repetitions. If laws describe the shape of a history, we still owe an account of which descriptions deserve the name “law.” Lewis's best-system approach offers one answer by balancing simplicity and strength across true descriptions. Yet that invites another question: how much does the balance depend on the language and properties with which we describe the world? Our copying exercise can be posed without first adopting his answer.

A defender of governing laws can respond that two complete histories might look alike while differing in what governs them, or that only a governing structure explains why the copied particles follow those paths. This is a serious possibility, although it is difficult to turn the alleged difference into a check within the imagined copy. The descriptive proposal also faces its own difficulty: if everything is fixed by the complete history, it must explain how scientific laws can legitimately guide expectations about unobserved parts of it and why some true generalizations are merely accidental. Neither challenge disappears because the thought experiment is vivid.

For now, the experiment yields a question rather than a verdict. When we say a law is *there*, are we pointing to a feature of the entire arrangement of events, to something that produces that arrangement, or to a disciplined way of describing it? Our answer may depend partly on whether we take a history to be complete or to come into being. The world encountered in experience unfolds with remarkable order. Calling that order a law helps us find our way through it. Why it has this particular form remains a further question.
