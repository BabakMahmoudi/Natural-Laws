# Thingness, the Mosaic, and Explanatory Tolerance

*AI-generated dialogue (model: GPT-6), edited from an actual exchange with Babak on 18 November 2026. Babak affirmed the central position reached in the exchange and requested this record; he has not yet reviewed this edited text.*

**Editorial note:** The exchange is lightly edited and condensed for readability, preserving the order and attribution of its turns. “Humean” and “Carnapian” mark philosophical affinities explored in the conversation, not a claim that its conclusions reproduce either philosopher's complete position. The literature comparisons remain provisional; no new primary-source verification was undertaken for this record. The electron example isolates a philosophical question and does not purport to give a complete physical definition of an electron.

**Babak:** Let us think about the mode of being of a law. It feels natural to see a particle and assert that there is a particle: we can point to it. What changes when we try to believe that, out there, there is Coulomb's law?

Suppose, in a radical move beyond Bird's essentialism, I posit that the very essence of an electron is its behavior in an electric field. Even then, I tend to say that an electron is a thing that behaves in this way. I cannot get rid of thingness. Behavior always seems to have to be assigned to a thing, even when I want to insist on behavior as a mode of being independently of the thing.

**GPT-6:** You are asking whether behavior can itself exist, or whether it must always be the behavior of something.

When we say that there is a particle, we treat it as an individual: something we can locate, track, and distinguish from another particle. Saying that there is Coulomb's law seems different. We are claiming that a relationship holds across interactions. Whether that relationship requires a further entity to make it hold is the metaphysical question.

Your electron proposal permits three formulations. First, there is a thing that has electrical behavior. Second, being this thing consists in having a pattern of possible behavior. Third, there is an organized pattern of events that we identify as an electron.

The second formulation removes a characterless bearer underneath the properties, while retaining an individual constituted by those properties. An individual need not be a bare something beneath its characteristics.

The third makes the more radical move. Yet “behavior” may already suggest a subject that behaves. Beginning with events, interactions, or processes leaves more room to ask how an enduring particle is identified within them.

There is an explanatory debt here. What makes these events one electron's history rather than an arbitrary collection? Continuity, organization, or relations to other events might provide an answer, but naming a pattern does not yet establish its unity.

We must also distinguish what the electron actually does from what it would do in circumstances it never encounters. The latter introduces unactualized possibilities. If these belong to its essence, the question about laws reappears within its identity: what makes those possible responses part of what it is?

Your block-universe proposal suggests one formulation: what exists is an organized history of events; an electron is an identifiable continuity within that history; a law expresses a regularity or symmetry of its organization. That is a proposal to examine, rather than a result already established by our discussion.

The question is therefore whether thingness must be fundamental or can arise from the organization of events. You may need individuals without needing an additional substance underneath them.

**Babak:** Yes, it feels so to me. And that is classified as Humean, right?

**GPT-6:** Your view has a Humean character, particularly if the actual history of events is all that needs to exist and laws describe its patterns without adding something that governs it.

But we should distinguish two questions. Are things fundamental, or do they emerge from patterns of events? And does reality contain necessary connections that determine what can happen? Giving events priority does not by itself settle the second question.

In the electron example, the meaning of “essence” matters. Identifying the electron through its actual interactions fits the direction you are taking. Positing a power that necessarily determines its responses even in situations that never occur moves toward dispositional essentialism.

Your view of laws as descriptions of patterns or symmetries in the actual block universe belongs broadly on the Humean side. It does not thereby commit you to Lewis's particular best-system account. You can accept the mosaic while questioning how its descriptions are selected and formulated.

**Babak:** Yes. I tend to stand at this position. All we can claim is a Humean mosaic. Above that, all positions are frameworks of explanation, which should be approached in a Carnapian mood of tolerance and use.

**GPT-6:** That gives your position two levels: a modest account of what exists and a tolerant attitude toward how we explain it.

At the first level, you accept actual occurrences and their arrangement—the mosaic. At the second, you assess governing laws, dispositions, causal powers, and other explanatory frameworks through what they help us understand and accomplish. Their usefulness does not by itself establish the additional structures they posit.

I would qualify “all we can claim.” Saying that only the mosaic exists is itself a substantial metaphysical claim. Your position sounds more cautious: the mosaic is what you presently accept, while withholding commitment about anything further that grounds its regularities.

Tolerance need not make frameworks equally good. We can compare clarity, empirical adequacy, predictive success, and usefulness for particular questions. Accepting a framework for a purpose can be distinguished from believing its ontology.

I would formulate the position this way:

> My present ontological commitment extends to actual occurrences and their arrangement. I regard accounts of laws, powers, and necessary connections as explanatory frameworks whose adoption should depend on their adequacy and usefulness. Their success warrants using them, while further argument is needed to establish their additional ontological commitments.

This leaves thingness open. You can describe the mosaic through particles, events, or processes while investigating whether any one vocabulary reveals its fundamental constitution.

**Babak:** Yes, that is all I can believe.
