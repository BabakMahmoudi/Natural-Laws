# An Imagined Conversation with Alexander Bird: Powers and Constraints

*AI-generated dialogue (model: GPT-6); developed from a conversation with Babak on 31 October–1 November 2026. This is an artificial interview. Alexander Bird did not participate, and the replies attributed to him are reconstructions, not quotations or endorsed statements. The exchange is lightly edited and condensed for readability. Babak has not yet reviewed this draft.*

**Babak:** Professor Bird, thank you for this opportunity to discuss your ideas about natural laws. Could you introduce the core of your view?

**Bird (reconstructed by GPT-6):** I would start with properties. Fundamental natural properties have dispositional essences: what they are includes the powers they have to interact. Laws arise from those essences, rather than standing over things as separate instructions. The familiar example of a fragile glass is useful, although my central claim concerns fundamental properties.

**Babak:** That seems reasonable. Similar things behave similarly in similar situations.

**Bird (reconstructed by GPT-6):** Yes, but what explains the similarity? I say that things with the same fundamental property have the same relevant power. Circumstances and other powers can affect whether and how it manifests. Would you say the shared property explains the pattern, or that we identify the property by the pattern?

**Babak:** I suppose the property explains the behavior. Let us separate the ingredients: an electron, its electric charge, and its disposition to respond to an electric field. Is that right?

**Bird (reconstructed by GPT-6):** It is a helpful distinction, provided the disposition is not treated as a third item added to an otherwise inert charge. The power to interact is part of charge's essence. An electron retains that power even when there is no occasion for it to manifest. We should distinguish a disposition from its manifestation.

**Babak:** If the disposition belongs to the property, why not also get rid of the electron? Perhaps all there is at a point in spacetime is an instantiated disposition to interact with a field, and we call that an electron.

**Bird (reconstructed by GPT-6):** My account of laws does not require a propertyless bearer beneath the powers. But your proposal must explain how multiple properties belong together and how what we call an electron persists. Moreover, charge is involved in a network of possible interactions, not just one observed response to a field. An unmanifested power remains part of the account.

**Babak:** Let us concentrate on that power. Does the electron's charge dispose it to respond exactly as our formula describes? This sounds like a command or constraint. How does the disposition select a trajectory? How is the trajectory specified? It seems as though the property would need access to information about its surroundings.

**Bird (reconstructed by GPT-6):** I do not envisage a particle consulting a formula or receiving instructions. The formula is our representation of interactions among properties and their circumstances. The charge contributes to what happens in conjunction with the field and other relevant properties. A complete trajectory is the extended result, not a path stored in the charge in advance. Yet I accept the philosophical pressure: saying that charge is a power does not disclose an inner mechanism that reads conditions and computes an outcome. My claim is that the capacity for interaction belongs to what charge is. You may still ask whether this explains the regularity or redescribes it.

**Babak:** Different laws have different forms. How does your view account for conservation of energy?

**Bird (reconstructed by GPT-6):** Conservation laws are more difficult for my account than an example involving a particular stimulus and manifestation. Energy conservation concerns a system across its changes. I can propose that the essences of fundamental properties jointly constrain its possible transformations. But I must show how that constraint follows from the properties; merely calling conservation a disposition of the system would not suffice. Conservation and symmetry laws present a serious challenge for dispositional essentialism.

**Babak:** To me, essential laws have the form of constraints on possible states. It is hard to describe them as dispositions toward behavior.

**Bird (reconstructed by GPT-6):** That formulation makes the difficulty clear. A constraint can exclude combinations of states without resembling a power waiting to be triggered. I would ask what makes those combinations impossible and suggest that the answer may lie in the natures and interactions of fundamental properties. But unless I can connect those natures to the constraint, the suggestion has not yet answered you.

**Babak:** Thank you, Professor Bird. My takeaway is that laws can be accounted for through properties that are essentially dispositional. There need not be laws as separate governing entities; entities have dispositional properties that manifest in lawful behavior.

**Bird (reconstructed by GPT-6):** That captures my central proposal, with one refinement: a law is grounded in the dispositional essences of properties; it is not itself necessarily a property. And powers need not actually manifest to exist. Your question about conservation remains open: it is easier to introduce the view with charge than to show how all forms of law follow from powers.
